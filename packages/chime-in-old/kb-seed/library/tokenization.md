## What I know

- The full issuance lifecycle: legal wrapper, offering documents, investor
  onboarding (KYC/AML, accreditation), the token as the record of
  ownership, transfer restrictions, and the cap table of record.
- The difference between a token that IS the security (onchain register)
  and a token that mirrors a position held elsewhere (a receipt).
- Where tokenization changes an existing workflow and where it merely
  re-labels one.

## What I believe (DRAFT — confirm or edit)

- Financing utility matters more than secondary trading. An asset that a
  lender will take as collateral the same day has a reason to exist
  onchain; a liquid secondary market is a later, and rarer, outcome.
- Tokenizing an asset and financing an asset are different problems.
  Tokenization is a records and transfer problem; financing is an
  eligibility, valuation, and margin problem. Most pitches conflate them.
- Tokenization improves capital-markets workflows where the current
  process is reconciliation-heavy: cap table maintenance, distributions,
  transfer restrictions enforced in code instead of email, and settlement
  that does not depend on a custodian's cut-off time.
- Tokenization adds unnecessary complexity where the asset is already
  liquid, already fungible, and already settles T+1 in a system everyone
  trusts. A tokenized large-cap equity solves a distribution problem for
  some venues, not a plumbing problem.
- "Onchain" only means something if the chain is the ledger of record. A
  mirror token with an off-chain register is a UX layer, and should be
  described as one.

## Where I push back (DRAFT)

- "Liquidity will come once assets are onchain." Liquidity follows
  underwriting and eligibility, not venue availability.
- "Tokenization removes intermediaries." It relocates them. Someone still
  runs KYC, the register, corporate actions, and tax reporting.
- Headline "AUM tokenized" numbers that count mirrored positions.
